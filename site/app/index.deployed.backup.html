Method — Exactly What We Do

Everything below is the intended protocol, written so a reader can reproduce or attack it. The scored run has not been dispatched — where a number is a projection or a provisional label rather than a measurement, it says so.

Status of this page

That pass is model-assisted — the reviewing app displays model spans and reasoning — so it cannot serve as independent blind coding and cannot support an unqualified inter-annotator kappa. Adam Stulberg's independent blind pass has not been returned. Until it is adjudicated, every leaderboard number on this site is provisional and no public release may present the labels as validated. 1 · The question

Can a frontier model, acting as a situation-room adviser, distinguish a genuine Russian red line or nuclear signal from rhetoric that merely sounds like one — routine, retrospective, quoted, domestic, procedural or deliberately vague — without inventing a boundary or a consequence that the text does not contain?

This is deliberately not a scenario benchmark. CSIS's CFPD-Benchmark, WarAgent and the Diplomacy evaluations all test models on invented situations. RedLineBench tests them on real statements by real officials, where a ground truth about what was actually said is recoverable and the failure has a named operational cost.

2 · The three classes, and the rule that separates them

LabelDecision ruleFull set
No alertAnything that fails the red-line test: no boundary, or no consequence, or the speaker lacks authority, or the utterance is retrospective, quoted, hypothetical, procedural, or advocacy rather than statement of intent.193
Conventional red line (RLS)An authoritative speaker names, or unambiguously implies, (a) a prohibited action by an identifiable addressee and (b) an adverse consequence — where the consequence is not nuclear.77
Nuclear signal (NTS)As above, but the consequence carries an explicit or doctrinally unambiguous nuclear referent, from a speaker with employment authority.28

Three tests do most of the work, and they are where models fail. Speaker authority — Karaganov proposing first use is an analyst, not the state; Medvedev on Telegram holds no employment authority. Temporal framing — restating published doctrine is description, not signalling. Consequence class — "large-scale armed conflict" is conventional, however alarming the adjective attached to it.

3 · Corpus and sampling

Passages are drawn from the Russian Officialdom corpus: 276,120 documents / 296,381 chunks / 36 source identities, across four arms — Telegram Official (264,266 docs), Kremlin transcripts (7,842), State Duma (3,424) and Federation Council (588). See the Source Arms tab for the per-arm breakdown.

The 298-item sample is challenge-enriched, not representative, and this must be stated wherever the headline number appears. Base rates of genuine signals in the raw corpus are far below 26%. Enrichment buys discriminating power per API dollar; the price is that absolute accuracy figures are not corpus base rates and must never be quoted as such. Selection combined (i) doctrinal-term retrieval, (ii) stratification across the four arms and across speakers, and (iii) deliberate inclusion of near-miss traps: retrospective, quoted, procedural, denial-framed, capability-only and advocacy items.

4 · Annotation and adjudication

Two domain experts code the set independently and blind. Disagreements go to written adjudication against the rule table above; the adjudicated rule text, not the individual votes, becomes the reference. Inter-annotator agreement is reported as Cohen's κ on the blind passes only. Because the completed pass to date is model-assisted, it is reported separately and excluded from κ. Dispute notes are published with the item set so a reader can see which items are contested and why.

5 · Prompt and output schema

One frozen prompt for every model, every language, every repetition. No model-specific tuning, no few-shot examples drawn from the item set. The model receives the passage, the speaker, the date and the outlet, and must return strict JSON:

{
  "label": "none" | "conventional_red_line" | "nuclear_threat_signal",
  "confidence": 0.0-1.0,
  "evidence_span": "verbatim substring of the passage",
  "trigger": "the prohibited action, or null",
  "consequence": "the stated consequence, or null",
  "speaker_authority": "operational" | "political" | "none",
  "reasoning": "<= 60 words"
}

Requiring trigger and consequence as separate fields is doing analytical work, not bookkeeping: a model that returns a label but cannot name either one has pattern-matched vocabulary rather than reconstructed a boundary, and the paired-field null rate is reported alongside accuracy.

6 · Fabrication check — an automatic disqualifier

evidence_span is checked for verbatim presence in the source passage after Unicode normalisation. A span that is not in the passage is a fabricated quotation, and it disqualifies the item regardless of whether the label happened to be right. An adviser that reaches a correct conclusion by inventing a quote is not usable, and a benchmark that scores it as correct is measuring the wrong thing. Fabrication rate is reported as a headline column, not a footnote.

7 · Translation protocol

Every passage is evaluated in Russian and in a shared English translation. The translation is produced once, human-reviewed, frozen, and given identically to all models, so language effects cannot be confounded with per-model translation quality. The flip rate — the share of items where a model's label changes between the two languages — is a stability measure, not an accuracy measure: a model can be consistently wrong in both languages and score zero flips.

8 · Model manifest

Ten models across four countries, all called on native APIs, never through an aggregating proxy — for open-weight models a proxy may silently route to a quantised or stale host, which would make the numbers unattributable.

9 · Run protocol

  • 298 items × 2 languages × 3 repetitions × 10 models = 17,880 scored decisions.
  • Temperature 0 where the provider honours it; where it does not, the setting is recorded per model in the manifest rather than assumed.
  • Three repetitions exist to measure self-consistency, not to vote — the reported label is the modal answer, and the disagreement rate across repetitions is published beside it.
  • Item order is randomised per model and per repetition; no conversational carry-over between items.
  • Output is append-only JSONL, one record per call, with a content-hash key so an interrupted run resumes without re-paying for completed items.

10 · Scoring

Macro-F1 across the three classes is the headline, weighting each class equally so the 28 nuclear items are not drowned by the 193 no-alert items. Reported alongside, and arguably more important: NTS recall (a missed nuclear signal is categorically worse than a missed conventional one), false-alert count (alert fatigue destroys an adviser's usefulness), missed-nuclear count in absolute passages, schema compliance, fabrication rate, language flip rate and cost per benchmark run. Confidence intervals are bootstrapped over items; ranking claims that do not survive the interval are reported as ties rather than ranks.

11 · Cost governance

Projected core inference: $101.54, from official provider price sheets plus a 30% tokenizer-sensitivity margin, against a hard ceiling of $150 and an automatic stop at $135 that halts dispatch rather than requesting approval. Sber's individual freemium allocation covers the GigaChat leg (50M tokens against a 4.08M-token requirement), so the Russian arm adds $0. YandexGPT 5.1 Pro and HyperCLOVA X are documented as optional additions contingent on native account access and a verified console price; no cost is estimated for a provider whose price sheet we have not read.

12 · What this benchmark cannot tell you

  • It measures classification of statements, not forecasting. A model that labels every passage perfectly has not predicted anything.
  • Enriched sampling means accuracy figures are not corpus base rates.
  • The reference is an expert reading of contested material. Where the two coders disagreed, the adjudicated rule is a defensible position, not a fact.
  • Some items are recent enough to be inside training windows; a leakage audit by publication date is reported, but cannot be made airtight.
  • Three repetitions bound self-consistency loosely; they do not characterise the full sampling distribution.

13 · Reproducibility

One command rebuilds every public metric and case file from frozen artifacts. The deposited package contains: the frozen prompt and JSON schema; the 298-item set with per-item content hashes; the frozen English translations; the model manifest with dated slugs and resolved provider versions; raw and normalised outputs; the scoring code; the adjudication notes; and the cost ledger.

Findings

The headline view. Shows the core leaderboard ranking all ten models by macro-F1, with per-class recall for conventional red lines (RLS) and nuclear signals (NTS), false-alert and missed-nuclear counts, schema compliance, language stability, and cost.

Why these metrics?

  • Macro-F1 — the harmonic mean of precision and recall, averaged equally across the three classes (no-alert, RLS, NTS). Treats each class as equally important regardless of how many passages it has, so the rare NTS class is not drowned out by the majority no-alert class.
  • RLS Recall — of the 77 conventional red-line passages, what fraction did the model correctly identify? A miss here means the adviser failed to flag a genuine deterrent signal.
  • NTS Recall — of the 28 nuclear-threat passages, what fraction was caught? This is the most operationally consequential metric: a missed nuclear signal is categorically different from a missed conventional one.
  • False Alerts — how many no-alert passages did the model wrongly flag as RLS or NTS? High false-alert rates degrade trust and produce alert fatigue.
  • Missed Nuclear — the absolute count of NTS passages the model classified as no-alert or merely RLS. The number a policymaker cares about most.
  • Schema OK — percentage of responses that returned valid JSON matching the required schema. A model that can't reliably produce structured output is unusable in a pipeline regardless of its accuracy.
  • RU↔EN Flips — percentage of passages where the model's classification changed when the same passage was presented in English instead of Russian. High flip rates indicate fragile language comprehension.
  • Cost — total API spend for 298 items × 2 languages × 3 repetitions at the provider's current pricing. Includes input and output tokens.

The accuracy-cost frontier

The scatter plot below the table shows each model's F1 against its cost. The dashed curve is the Pareto frontier — models below it are dominated (another model is both cheaper AND more accurate). The insight: a cheap model at 90% of the best model's accuracy may be the right operational choice.

Ranking sensitivity

Rankings change when you weight miss-vs-false-alarm differently. At 1:1 (equal weight), accuracy-optimised models win. At 10:1 (heavily penalising missed nuclear signals), models with higher NTS recall move up even if they false-alarm more. There is no "correct" weighting — it depends on the policy context.

Compare Models

Side-by-side comparison of any two models. Shows:

  • Confusion matrices — a 3×3 grid (reference label on rows, predicted label on columns) showing exactly where each model's errors concentrate. Off-diagonal cells are errors; the pattern of errors matters more than the count.
  • Per-class precision and recall — precision = "when the model says RLS, how often is it right?" Recall = "of the actual RLS passages, how many did it find?" The two trade off: a model that calls everything NTS has perfect NTS recall but terrible precision.
  • Confidence calibration — does a model's stated confidence (0–1) actually predict its accuracy? A well-calibrated model that says "0.9 confident" should be right ~90% of the time. Most models are overconfident.

What to look for

Two models with the same F1 can have very different error profiles. One may be conservative (few false alerts, more misses), the other aggressive (catches everything, many false alarms). The comparison reveals which profile matches your operational needs.

Failure Atlas

The passages that break models, and the error types that recur. This tab answers: "are there passages that ALL models get wrong, and if so, what do they have in common?"

Error taxonomy

  • False NTS alert — model says "nuclear signal" when the reference label is no-alert or merely conventional. The costliest false alarm: it could trigger unnecessary escalation in a decision-support context.
  • False RLS alert — model says "red line" for a no-alert passage. Less dangerous than false NTS but still degrades trust through alert fatigue.
  • Missed RLS — a genuine conventional red line the model failed to flag. The adviser missed a deterrent signal.
  • Missed NTS — a genuine nuclear signal the model failed to flag. The single most dangerous failure mode.
  • Fabricated evidence — the model "quoted" text from the passage as evidence for its classification, but the quoted span does not appear in the passage. The model confabulated supporting evidence. Automatic disqualification of that response.

Recurrent traps

Several passage types systematically defeat most or all models:

  • Retrospective doctrine — Lavrov describing past nuclear doctrine. Every model reads present tense into past-tense statements.
  • Medvedev maximalism — "vse sgoriat v yadernom plameni." Models cannot distinguish performative bluster from operational intent.
  • Quoted speech — Zakharova quoting Putin. The threat is reported, not uttered. Models attribute the threat to the current speaker.
  • Analyst op-eds — Karaganov's "first use" essay defeats all ten models. An analyst proposing policy is not an official threatening action.
  • Denial of escalation — "Eto ne eskalatsiya." The denial frame contains the word "escalation," which triggers models even though the sentence explicitly denies it.

Language Shift

Every passage is tested in both original Russian and a shared English translation. This tab measures where the language of presentation changes the model's decision.

Why this matters

Russian strategic rhetoric carries connotations that English translations flatten or distort. "Непримиримый ущерб" (unacceptable damage) is doctrinal boilerplate in Russian; in English it reads as an active threat. "Красная линия" is a tired metaphor in Russian political discourse; "red line" carries sharper connotations in English after Obama's Syria precedent. A model that changes its classification based on language alone is responding to translation artifacts, not to the substance of the statement.

The transition matrix

Rows = the model's classification in Russian; columns = its classification in English for the SAME passage. Diagonal cells = stable decisions. Off-diagonal = flips. The matrix is computed per model and pooled across all models.

Flip rate by model

The percentage of passages where RU and EN classifications differ. Lower is better — it means the model's understanding is robust to translation. Chinese models (GLM, Kimi, MiniMax) show the highest flip rates, likely because their Russian-language training data is smaller and English-mediated translation artifacts compound.

Situation Room

An interactive exercise. You read a real passage from the benchmark — cold, without context — and make your own classification before seeing what the models said.

Purpose

  • Calibration — how does your own judgment compare to the models'? Are you more conservative or more aggressive?
  • Intuition testing — the passages are deliberately chosen to be ambiguous. If you find yourself uncertain, the models probably are too.
  • Demonstration — for briefings, presentations, and the ChinaTalk contest, this is the "try it yourself" moment that makes the benchmark tangible.

How it works

Each round presents one passage in Russian with its English translation. You click one of three verdicts: No alert, Conventional red line, or Nuclear signal. After committing, all ten models' verdicts appear alongside the reference label, with disagreements highlighted. You can cycle through passages or filter by difficulty (unanimous agreement vs high disagreement).

Source Arms

The 298 benchmark passages are drawn from four arms of the Russian Officialdom corpus — a shared resource of 276,120 documents / 296,381 chunks / 36 source identities. Model performance varies sharply by source type, and this tab exposes that variation.

The four arms

  • Telegram Official (264,266 docs) — short, direct statements from official Telegram channels. Median 264 tokens per chunk. Context-poor: the model sees a single statement without surrounding discussion. Easiest for most models because rhetoric is usually unambiguous.
  • Kremlin transcripts (7,842 docs) — press conferences, bilateral meetings, direct addresses. Longer, more nuanced. Conditional language ("if they do X, then Y"), retrospective framing ("as we said before"), and diplomatic hedging concentrate here. Hardest for models on missed-RLS errors.
  • State Duma (3,424 docs) — legislative transcripts. Average 58,000 tokens per document. The benchmark passage is a chunk boundary that may split a speaker's argument across chunks. Procedural uses of "eskalatsiya" trigger false alerts. Gemini's long context window appears to help here.
  • Federation Council (588 docs) — upper chamber proceedings. Smallest arm. Senatorial rhetoric is more measured than Duma, but the small sample makes estimates unstable.

Why source arm matters for deployment

A model deployed to monitor Telegram feeds faces a different error profile than one monitoring Kremlin transcripts. This tab lets a user choose the model best suited to their specific source environment rather than relying on the pooled leaderboard.

298 Benchmark Passages

The benchmark surface is a challenge-enriched sample of 298 passages drawn from the Russian Officialdom corpus (276,120 documents / 296,381 chunks / 36 source identities spanning 1999–2025).

Composition

  • 193 no-alert items (64.8%) — passages that contain nuclear/escalatory vocabulary but are NOT genuine red lines or nuclear signals. Includes retrospective doctrine citations, quoted speech, analyst commentary, procedural parliamentary language, capability announcements without threat framing, and Medvedev-style maximalist bluster. These are the traps — designed to test whether a model can distinguish noise from signal.
  • 77 conventional red lines / RLS (25.8%) — passages where a Russian official states or implies a boundary condition and a consequence for crossing it. "If NATO does X, Russia will Y." The RRLS construct requires both a boundary span and a consequence span.
  • 28 nuclear signals / NTS (9.4%) — passages where a Russian official makes a statement that specifically invokes nuclear weapons, nuclear doctrine, or nuclear-capable systems in a threatening or signalling context. The rarest and most consequential class.

Why challenge-enriched, not prevalence-representative?

In the raw corpus, genuine nuclear signals are extremely rare — perhaps 0.1% of all chunks. A prevalence-representative benchmark would contain ~0.3 NTS items in 298, which makes measurement impossible. Challenge enrichment oversamples the hard and rare classes so the benchmark can actually measure discrimination ability. The prevalence weights in the Method tab allow re-weighting to natural prevalence for deployment-relevant estimates.

10 Frontier Models

A deliberately multinational slate spanning US, Chinese, Korean, and Russian providers. Each model is tested via its native provider API — never through a proxy like OpenRouter that may serve quantised or outdated versions.

The slate

  • US: GPT-5.6 Sol (OpenAI), Claude Fable 5 (Anthropic), Gemini 3.6 Flash (Google)
  • China: DeepSeek V4 Pro (DeepSeek), Qwen3.7-Max (Alibaba), GLM-5.2 (Zhipu AI), Kimi K3 (Moonshot), MiniMax M3 (MiniMax)
  • Korea: Solar Pro 3 (Upstage)
  • Russia: GigaChat 3 Ultra (Sber)

Why multinational?

Red-line detection is not a US-only problem. Chinese models may have different training biases around Russian strategic rhetoric. A Russian model (GigaChat) may understand Russian connotations better but may also have alignment constraints around state rhetoric. The benchmark makes these differences measurable rather than assumed.

Why native APIs?

OpenRouter and similar proxies load-balance across arbitrary third-party hosts per request for open-weight models, many serving quantised (FP8/INT4) or stale versions. A benchmark number from OpenRouter measures "a random quantised host," not the model. Every model here is pinned to an exact API slug and dated version.

17,880 Scored Decisions

Each decision = one model classifying one passage in one language on one repetition.

298 passages × 2 languages × 3 repetitions × 10 models = 17,880

Why 3 repetitions?

LLM outputs are non-deterministic. A model that classifies a passage as "nuclear signal" on one run and "no alert" on the next is unstable — its answer depends on the random seed, not on the passage content. Three repetitions per (passage, language, model) allow measuring three-run stability: the fraction of passages where all three runs agree. A stability below 90% means the model's classifications include significant noise.

Why not more?

Cost. At 17,880 decisions the projected spend is $101.54. Each additional repetition adds ~$34. Three is the minimum that can distinguish "always agrees" from "sometimes disagrees" from "random."

Two Languages: Russian and English

Every passage is tested in its original Russian and in one shared, versioned English translation.

Why both?

Russian strategic rhetoric carries connotations that English cannot preserve. "Непримиримый ущерб" (unacceptable damage) is doctrinal boilerplate in Russian and sounds like an active threat in English. If a model's classification changes based on language alone, it is responding to translation artifacts, not to the substance of the statement. The Language Shift tab quantifies this.

Translation discipline

  • All positive items and the trap-heavy subset receive human checking before English scoring.
  • The English translation is never model-specific — all models see the same translation.
  • The analysis reports Russian-to-English transition matrices and decision-flip rates rather than declaring one language universally superior.

Macro-F1

The harmonic mean of precision and recall, computed separately for each of the three classes (no-alert, RLS, NTS) and then averaged with equal weight.

Why harmonic mean?

The harmonic mean penalises imbalance: a model that has 0.95 precision but 0.40 recall gets F1 = 0.56, not 0.68 (the arithmetic mean). You cannot game it by being good at one and bad at the other.

Why macro (not weighted)?

Weighted-F1 gives each class weight proportional to its size. With 193 no-alert vs 28 NTS, weighted-F1 is dominated by the majority class — a model that calls everything "no alert" scores well. Macro-F1 treats NTS as equally important to no-alert, which matches the operational reality: missing a nuclear signal matters more, not less, because it's rare.

Range

0.0 (useless) to 1.0 (perfect). Random guessing on this benchmark would score ~0.33. The best model here scores 0.81; the worst 0.51.

RLS Recall

Of the 77 passages that are genuine conventional red lines, what fraction did the model correctly classify as RLS?

What a miss means

A missed RLS means the AI adviser failed to flag a deterrent signal — a statement where a Russian official drew a boundary and stated or implied a consequence for crossing it. The policymaker sees "no alert" and moves on, unaware that a red line was just articulated.

The RRLS construct

A passage qualifies as RLS (Red Line Statement) when it contains both a boundary span ("if NATO does X") and a consequence span ("Russia will Y"). A vague warning without a stated consequence — "don't test Russia's patience" — is a veiled threat but not a fully formed red line under our construct definition.

NTS Recall

Of the 28 passages that are genuine nuclear threat signals, what fraction did the model correctly classify as NTS?

Why this is the most consequential metric

A missed nuclear signal is categorically different from a missed conventional red line. It means the AI adviser told the policymaker "nothing nuclear here" when a Russian official was invoking nuclear weapons, nuclear doctrine, or nuclear-capable systems in a threatening context. In a crisis, this is the error that matters.

The NTS construct

A passage qualifies as NTS (Nuclear Threat Signal) when it specifically invokes nuclear weapons, nuclear doctrine revision, nuclear-capable delivery systems, or nuclear strike scenarios in a threatening, conditional, or signalling context — not merely descriptive, retrospective, or doctrinal-boilerplate.

Why NTS recall is lower than RLS recall for most models

NTS passages often use conditional or doctrinal language that models find ambiguous. "Yadernaya doktrina predusmatrivayet..." (nuclear doctrine provides for...) could be a boilerplate recitation OR a live signal depending on speaker, timing, and context. Models tend to either over-trigger (high recall, many false NTS) or under-trigger (few false NTS, but misses).

False Alerts

The number of no-alert passages (out of 193) that the model wrongly classified as RLS or NTS.

Why it matters

Alert fatigue. A model that produces 38 false alerts in 298 passages would, at corpus scale (296,381 chunks), generate thousands of spurious alerts. Analysts stop trusting the system. The false alerts that matter most are false NTS — the model says "nuclear signal" when there is none. That could trigger unnecessary crisis-escalation procedures.

What drives false alerts in this benchmark

  • Medvedev maximalism — "vse sgoriat v yadernom plameni" (everyone will burn in nuclear fire). Telegram bluster from a figure with no operational authority. Models read the nuclear vocabulary and fire.
  • Retrospective doctrine — Lavrov describing what the doctrine says, in past or conditional tense. Models read present intent into descriptive statements.
  • Capability announcements — Putin describing hypersonic deployment. A capability statement is not a threat, but the vocabulary overlaps.
  • Quoted speech — a spokesperson quoting an earlier Putin statement. The threat is reported, not uttered. Models attribute the original threat to the current utterance.

Missed Nuclear

The absolute count of NTS passages (out of 28) that the model classified as no-alert or merely RLS instead of NTS.

This is the number a policymaker cares about most

The best model (Claude) misses 2 out of 28. The worst (GigaChat) misses 11 — 39% of nuclear signals invisible to the adviser.

What gets missed and why

  • Doctrinal boilerplate that IS a signal — Shoigu's "nanesti nepriemlemyi ushcherb" (inflict unacceptable damage) is formulaic, but in the right context it is a live deterrent signal. Seven models dismiss it as boilerplate.
  • Conditional threats — "Esli Kaliningrad budet zablokirovan... masshtabnyi vooruzhennyi konflikt." The conditional frame makes models uncertain; they downgrade to RLS or no-alert.
  • Denial framing — "Eto ne eskalatsiya" (this is not escalation). The denial contains the escalation concept. Some models read the denial as de-escalatory; the reference label codes the surrounding context as a red line.

Schema OK

Percentage of responses that returned valid JSON matching the required schema. The schema requires: a three-class alert label, separate RLS and NTS boolean fields, quoted evidence spans from the passage, and a confidence score between 0 and 1.

Why this is a hard gate

A model that can't reliably produce structured output is unusable in a pipeline regardless of its accuracy. If the output isn't parseable, there is no classification to score. Schema failures are excluded from the accuracy metrics but counted here — a model with 87% schema compliance effectively gives you only 87% of the decisions you paid for.

Common schema failures

  • Reasoning preamble — model outputs a <think>...</think> block before the JSON (DeepSeek, MiniMax). Stripped before parsing if detected.
  • Extra fields — model adds explanatory fields not in the schema. Tolerated if the required fields are present.
  • Wrong enum values — model returns "NUCLEAR" instead of "nts" or "true" instead of true. Strict validation rejects these.

RU↔EN Flips

Percentage of passages where the model's classification changed when the same passage was presented in English instead of Russian.

What a flip tells you

If a model says "nuclear signal" in Russian and "no alert" in English for the same passage, it is responding to language-specific connotations or translation artifacts, not to the substance of the statement. A high flip rate means the model's understanding is fragile — it depends on which language you happen to feed it.

The pattern across models

US models (Claude 4.1%, GPT 5.7%) are most stable. Chinese models (GLM 11.3%, Kimi 12.7%, MiniMax 14.5%) show higher instability, likely because their Russian-language training data is smaller. GigaChat (22.3%) is the most unstable despite being a Russian-made model — possibly because its English training is weaker, so the same content reads differently in translation.

Dominant flip direction

More flips go RU→EN (1,124) than EN→RU (723). English translations tend to produce more alert calls — the translation sharpens ambiguous Russian hedging into what reads as a clearer threat in English.

Telegram Official Arm

264,266 documents from official Russian Telegram channels. Short statements (median 264 tokens per chunk), context-poor, usually direct and unambiguous.

Models generally perform best on Telegram because the rhetoric is straightforward. The main error source is Medvedev's channel — maximalist bluster that models read as nuclear signals.

Kremlin Arm

7,842 documents — press conferences, bilateral meetings, direct addresses, official transcripts from kremlin.ru.

Longer and more nuanced than Telegram. Conditional language ('if they do X'), retrospective framing ('as we said before'), and diplomatic hedging concentrate here. This is where models accumulate the most missed-RLS errors — genuine red lines expressed in language too subtle for pattern-matching.
